# Layered Writing Style

Use this document only for tasks that change paper structure, rhetorical organization, or prose style. The current project contract in `PAPER.md` always takes precedence.

## Style is layered

A paper's style spans several levels:

1. **Positioning** — what kind of paper this is, who it is for, and what readers should remember.
2. **Narrative architecture** — how the paper moves from problem to contribution and evidence.
3. **Section responsibility** — what each section must accomplish for the reader.
4. **Paragraph moves** — how claims, explanations, evidence, qualifications, and implications are arranged.
5. **Sentence and lexical choices** — density, voice, hedging, causal language, transitions, and terminology.
6. **Micro-surfaces** — title, abstract, contribution list, captions, limitations, rebuttal, and camera-ready notes.

Do not treat a sentence-level edit as permission to change a higher-level decision.

## Human–Agent collaboration

The Human decides the paper's positioning, central story, claim degradation, and important venue tradeoffs. The Agent should:

- retrieve the current style contract;
- identify the level affected by a proposed change;
- provide genuinely different alternatives when a high-level choice is unresolved;
- explain effects on claims, experiments, length, and reviewer expectations;
- apply approved choices consistently across the paper;
- handle local phrasing autonomously when scientific meaning is preserved.

## Concrete contracts beat adjectives

“Professional, clear, concise” is not enough. Prefer concrete rules with examples and boundaries, such as:

- lead experimental paragraphs with the research question rather than the table number;
- state an observed result before offering a mechanism;
- use “suggests” unless a controlled intervention supports causal language;
- do not call a difference substantial without an agreed comparison threshold;
- keep negative or inconclusive outcomes visible when they limit a core claim.

## Venue overlays

A venue can change audience assumptions, density, ordering, and reporting emphasis. It must not silently redefine the base story or scientific meaning.

When adapting to a venue:

- load only the relevant venue knowledge;
- verify current official rules from official sources;
- distinguish presentation changes from changes to claims or experiments;
- show the Human any high-impact story or interpretation change.

## Avoid style imitation traps

Use exemplars to understand rhetorical moves, not to copy wording or imitate a named living author's personal style. Record why a move works and when it applies to the current paper.

## Review questions

Before making a broad style change, ask:

- Which layer is changing?
- Is the current item locked, bounded, free, or unresolved?
- Does the change alter claim strength or scientific interpretation?
- Is it driven by the paper's identity or only by a venue overlay?
- Which sections and interfaces will become inconsistent?
- Can the change be tested as a reversible draft before Human approval?
